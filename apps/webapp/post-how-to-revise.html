Five revision habits that actually work

Rereading feels productive, but it teaches you surprisingly little. Here’s what to do instead, and exactly how to do it.

Study tips · The Knowzone team · 12 November 2026 · 8 minute read

Most students revise the same way. They open their notes or textbook, read a topic again, highlight the important lines, maybe copy them out neatly, and move on. It feels like work. The pages look familiar, and familiar feels like knowing.

Then the exam asks a question, and the answer won’t come. You can picture the page in your notes, but not what was on it.

That gap has a simple cause. Rereading puts information in front of your eyes. An exam asks you to pull it out of your memory, on your own, under time pressure. Those are different skills, and you get good at the one you practise.

Researchers who study learning have tested revision methods for decades. In a large review of ten popular techniques, psychologist John Dunlosky and his colleagues found that rereading and highlighting helped very little, while testing yourself and spacing out your study were the most useful of all. The five habits below are built on that kind of evidence. None of them needs special talent, and they work for JAMB, WAEC and NECO as well as for university exams.

1. Test yourself before you feel ready

This is the single most powerful habit, and the least popular. Instead of reading a topic again, close your notes and try to bring it back from memory. Write down everything you remember about, say, osmosis: the definition, an example, what happens to a plant cell in pure water. Then open your notes and check what you missed or got wrong.

The struggle to remember is not a sign that you’re failing. It’s the thing that makes the memory stronger. In a well-known experiment, students who read a passage once and then tested themselves remembered much more a week later than students who spent the same time reading it again and again, even though the rereaders felt more confident.

How to do it:

  • After reading a section, close the book and write a short summary from memory. Only then compare.
  • Turn your notes into questions. “Mitochondria” becomes “What do mitochondria do, and which cells have many of them?”
  • Use flashcards, but say or write the answer before you turn the card over. Peeking doesn’t count.
  • Do practice questions early, not only at the end. You don’t have to finish a topic before testing yourself on it.

2. Space it out instead of cramming

Cramming the night before can get you through tomorrow’s test, but most of it is gone within days. Spreading the same hours across several days makes knowledge last, because each time you come back after a gap, your brain has to work a little harder to find it.

A simple pattern works well: study a topic, come back to it the next day, then after three or four days, then after a week, then after a few weeks. Each review can be short, ten or fifteen minutes of self-testing is enough.

How to do it:

  • Keep a list of topics with the date you last reviewed each one. Each day, start with whatever is due.
  • Start revising weeks before the exam, not days. Even 30 minutes a day over six weeks beats a few all-night sessions.
  • Let flashcards do the scheduling for you. Cards you know well should come back less often, and cards you miss should come back soon.

3. Mix your topics

Textbooks usually teach one topic at a time, and their exercises practise that topic only. After twenty questions on simultaneous equations, you know every question needs the same method. That’s comfortable, but it isn’t what an exam is like. In the exam hall, the questions are mixed, and part of the challenge is recognising which method each question needs.

Mixing topics during practice (researchers call it interleaving) feels harder and slower. Students often think it’s working worse. But when the test comes, those who practised mixed questions usually do better, because they’ve learned to tell problems apart.

How to do it:

  • Once you’ve learned a few topics in a subject, practise them together. In Physics, mix motion, electricity and waves in one session.
  • Before solving each question, pause and ask: what kind of problem is this, and how do I know?
  • Full practice papers are naturally mixed, so they’re great for this once you’ve covered most of the syllabus.

4. Explain it in your own words

If you can explain an idea simply, out loud, to someone who doesn’t know it, you understand it. If you can’t, you’ve just found exactly what to study next. Explaining forces you to connect facts, fill gaps and ask “why?”, which is what turns memorised lines into real understanding.

How to do it:

  • Teach a topic to a friend, a younger brother or sister, or a study group. Let them ask questions.
  • Keep asking yourself “why?” and “how?”: Why does a steep concentration gradient speed up diffusion? How does a guard cell open a stoma?
  • Connect new ideas to things you already know, or to everyday life. Osmosis is why a salted cucumber goes soft.
  • If nobody is around, explain it to Kora, the AI tutor in Knowzone, and ask her to point out what you missed.

5. Practise under real exam conditions

Knowing the material is only part of the job. You also need to answer under time pressure, without notes, in the exam’s format. Students who only ever practise at home with their books open often meet the clock for the first time on exam day, and it costs them marks.

How to do it:

  • From about a month before the exam, sit full papers with a timer: no phone, no notes, no breaks you wouldn’t get in the hall.
  • Learn your pace. For JAMB, roughly how many minutes can you spend per question in each subject? Where do you lose time?
  • Mark the paper honestly and spend as long studying your mistakes as you spent on the paper. Every wrong answer shows you a topic to revisit.
  • Practise the exam’s rules too: shading answers neatly, reading every option, skipping and coming back.

Putting it together: a sample week

You don’t need hours every day. Here’s what one week of steady revision could look like for a student preparing for exams:

  • Monday: learn a new topic (40 minutes). Close the book and write what you remember (10 minutes).
  • Tuesday: 15 minutes of flashcards on yesterday’s topic and anything else due, then a new topic.
  • Wednesday: mixed practice questions from the last three topics you studied (30 minutes). Check every answer.
  • Thursday: explain two topics out loud to a friend or to Kora. Note anything you couldn’t explain.
  • Friday: review what went wrong this week, then flashcards.
  • Saturday: a timed practice paper under exam conditions, then go through every mistake.
  • Sunday: rest. Sleep is when your brain stores what you practised.

Mistakes to stop making

  • Highlighting everything. If half the page is yellow, nothing stands out, and highlighting isn’t remembering.
  • Copying notes neatly. It looks impressive but takes hours and teaches little. Summarise from memory instead.
  • Studying only what you already know. It feels good, but your marks grow in the topics you find hard.
  • Revising with your phone beside you. Every notification breaks your focus. Put it in another room for the session.
  • Giving up sleep. A tired brain learns badly and remembers worse. A good night’s sleep before the exam is part of revision.

Start today, not the night before

None of these habits is complicated, but they feel harder than rereading, and that’s exactly why they work. Pick one topic today. Close your notes, write down what you remember, and check yourself. Then come back to it tomorrow.
